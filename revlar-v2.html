§ 01 — The problem

When the evidence itself is in question.

Synthetic video, cloned voices, and fabricated images are now convincing enough to change a verdict, a headline, or a payout. The hard part is no longer sensing that something is off — it is proving it, on the record, to someone who will push back.

i.

One tool isn't proof

Any detection API returns a score. A single black-box number — no second opinion, no one accountable — won't survive a challenge.

ii.

The cost is one-way

Publish the fake, admit the forged exhibit, pay the fraudulent claim — and there is no taking it back. You decide once.

iii.

It must be defensible

A conclusion you can't explain or document is a liability. You need a verdict you can put your name on and stand behind.

§ 03 — The standard

Corroboration is what makes it hold.

No single detector decides anything at Revlar. Agreement across independent engines — confirmed by a person who is accountable for the call — is what turns a raw score into a verdict.

a. Multiple independent engines

Different models catch different artifacts. When they converge, confidence rises; when they don't, an analyst digs in rather than guessing.

b. A human is accountable

Every case is reviewed by an analyst who signs off. There is a name behind the verdict, not just an algorithm.

c. Documented, not asserted

You get the reasoning and the trail behind the verdict — a record that stands up to scrutiny instead of asking for trust.
