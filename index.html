The problem

When the media is the evidence, a guess isn't good enough.

Synthetic video, voice, and images are now convincing enough to change a verdict, a headline, or a payout. The hard part is no longer noticing something feels off — it's proving it, on the record.

01

One tool isn't proof

Any detection API can return a score. A single black-box number — with no second opinion and no one accountable for it — won't hold up when someone pushes back.

02

The cost of being wrong is one-way

Publish the fake, admit the forged evidence, pay the fraudulent claim — and there's no taking it back. These are decisions you only get to make once.

03

You need it to be defensible

A conclusion you can't explain or document is a liability. You need a verdict you can put your name on and stand behind in front of an editor, a court, or a board.

How it works

From a suspicious file to a verdict you can defend.

Four steps. The detection is the raw material — the corroboration, judgment, and documentation are what make it count.

01

Submit securely

03

04

Court-ready report

You receive a clear verdict, the supporting evidence, and a documented chain of custody you can act on.

Why the verdict holds

Corroboration is what makes it defensible.

No single detector decides anything at Revlar. Agreement across independent engines — confirmed by a person who is accountable for the call — is what turns a raw score into a verdict.

Multiple independent engines

Different models catch different artifacts. When they converge, confidence rises; when they don't, an analyst digs in rather than guessing.

A human is accountable

Every case is reviewed by an analyst who signs off on the conclusion. There's a name behind the verdict, not just an algorithm.

Documented, not asserted

You get the reasoning and the trail behind the verdict — the kind of record that stands up to scrutiny instead of asking for trust.
